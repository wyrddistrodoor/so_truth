Artificium // Sojourner Truth

Re-make / Re-module

MARYA ERRIN JONES · MAY 21, 2026

“The truth is powerful and will prevail.”SOJOURNER TRUTH · BOOK OF LIFE · 1863

Abstract

This short essay is an attempt to engage with sound (noise) performance as an ontological experiment in order to “touch” upon a lived experience of 19th-century abolitionist, orator, and feminist, Sojourner Truth as a creative being. Obsessed with the photo images of Sojourner Truth since I was a little girl, I learned as an adult that she had lived most of life with a devastating, corporeal loss: her right index finger had been severed from her body. Truth, born into slavery in 1797, escaped to freedom with her infant daughter in 1826, she embarked on a life of lecturing, teaching, and craftmaking. Although Truth could neither read nor write, yet she engaged with two forms of technology to reclaim and remake herself in her own image. Through the ancient technology of knitting, and the modern technology of photography allowing for image reproduction (Carte de visite) Truth was able to provide for herself, and support her abolitionist and feminist practices on the world stage.

I will explore Truth’s uses of these technologies to express her knowledge and her value. Truth did more than emancipate herself, she used ancient and emerging technologies to regulate her system, to free others, and to create garments that illustrated her triumph over her body. I plan to observe my own struggles to knit, without using my right index finger.

How do you not know the Truth?

Recently, I was in the field, engaging in a wortcunning and herbalism apprenticeship. We followed our guide and instructor Kami, as he led us like ducklings through the Bosque. On my first journey into the woods, we spent three hours hiking on trails, cutting through high grass and the sandy floodplain of the Rio Grande, exploring and identifying native plants, endeavoring to build new relationships with local flora, and to learn their medical and magickal properties. While walking along a path, my dear friend, M-- and I started a conversation. She asked about my semester. I informed her that it was not yet over; that I had one more assignment to deliver. This experimental paper on Sojourner Truth.

I took several steps forward in my silence. I was not sure how to process this information from my friend, whom I know much about, but I didn’t know she was unaware of one of the first most famous Black women to walk the earth (alongside Harriet Tubman). Meanwhile, the woods continued to chirp, unaffected. Our footsteps continued to trample upon clumps of invasive grasses brought to the Bosque by 18th-century colonizers while we tip-toed around the young, sacred runners of the ancient paleo-herb, the Yerba Mansa plant that pre-dates the existence of the dinosaurs. However, something changed in me.

“I sell the shadow, to support the substance.”SOJOURNER TRUTH · 1864

My thoughts returned to Karen Barad’s engagement with the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, Michael Frayn’s exploration of the uncertainty principle in his play, Copenhagen as theorized in her book Meeting the Universe Halfway: Quantum Physics and the Entanglement of Matter and Meaning (2007). Frayn argues that theoretically, we can never know everything about human thinking. Barad argues that in Copenhagen, Frayn is not applying the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, “which concerns the limits to our knowledge of the behavior of physical objects, like atoms or electrons-to the problem of what it is possible to know about human behavior; he is simply drawing a parallel” (Barad, 2007).

I drew a parallel to my friend living her entire 30+ years never knowing of Sojourner Truth, and wondering how that was possible. I don’t believe her not knowing was intentional lack of knowledge, considering all that she has taught me about plants, performing music, and the martial arts, but I did realize the meaning behind Truth’s message she coined, etched below each photograph she signed for admirers and her critics, at her public appearances stating, “I sell the shadow, to support the substance.” (Truth, 1864). My guess is M-- had at least seen Sojourner Truth’s “shadow” in displays during Black History Month, at some point in her elementary school life, just as I had. And while I knew who she was, I had not reached through to her substance, or what Barad identifies as “entanglement” (Barad 2007). Just as the wortcunning crew were literally entangled with plants while wandering through the Bosque, looking at the herbal life we recognized, plants we had eaten, and received medicine derived from, we had not reached the true substance of them.

I had never looked that closely at her hands.

Throughout my life, I had experienced entanglement with Sojourner Truth and I was an individual, as Barad explains, [emerging] through and as part of [...] entangled intra-relating. Rather than continue looking at the images of Truth for another decade, (pictures that she paid to have taken, and then copyrighted those images) I decided to welcome the entanglement. It was then that I noticed her missing finger. I had never looked that closely at her hands. After conducting some research, I came to the understanding that Sojourner Truth was indeed an amputee. She was living without her right index finger. My child’s eyes were fixated on her facial neutrality, as I was just learning to read emotion through facial expressions, but I also focused on her knitwear. In every photo I remember, Truth wore a finely-knitted shawl, and often a knitted cap. Now, as an adult, it is a revelation to me that Truth was missing her right index finger, severed during her enslavement. How did she create intricate knitwork?

“Two hundred and forty-six years of outward submission during slavery time got folks to thinking of us as creatures of task alone. When in fact the conflict between what we wanted to do and what we were forced to do intensified our inner life instead of destroying it.”ZORA NEALE HURSTON · DUST TRACKS ON A ROAD

I knit therefore I purl.

I have attempted to learn to knit for at least 10 years. With my own two hands, I have made simple scarves, and a pair of fingerless gloves I can’t find. For me, knitting is a stressful form of leisure and not a necessity. However, I do realize that knitting has come with its own pattern recognition of exclusion. As a young person, I learned from scenes in films, and magazines on shelves that, Black women didn’t knit. According to the dissertation of Dana Williams-Johnson on the subject this is not a phenomenon. Although changes have been made to recognize the leisure lives of Black women, it comes with a color caveat:

“Since 2020, knitting magazines have increased the use of black women on their covers, but they typically feature black women with lighter complexion and soft hair, not showing the full range of blackness that exists. Through these stereotypical projections of black womanhood, knitting magazines reinforce stereotypes, and either project those stereotypes on their social media platforms or completely whitewash their social media and ignore diversity on those platforms.”DANA WILLIAMS-JOHNSON · 2023

Truth did not deal with knitting along on the colorline - she was charged to prove that knitting affirmed her humanity in the first place. She was not afforded an identity that acknowledged her femininity, hence her “Ain’t I a Woman?” speech (Truth, 1851). Her ability to express her humanness in an inhumane world with a piece of her missing, in a society that did not acknowledge her bodily autonomy or see her as fully human was something she would face throughout her public and private life. Katherine McKittrick argues in Demonic Grounds: Black Women and the Cartographies of Struggle that, “black women’s political feminist, imaginary and creative concerns, respatialize the graphic legacy of racism-sexism” (McKittrick, 2006). Knitting as a form of Black feminist technology allowed Truth to express her knowledge and her value. To create these knitted garments, Truth illustrated her triumph over her body. Through the instruction of knitting to others, Truth asserted her authority, over the artificium of knitting, beyond craft.

Did Sojourner Truth, in adapting to her circumstances, become a form of cyborg?

“A cyborg is a cybernetic organism, a hybrid of machine and organism, a creature of social reality as well as a creature of fiction”DONNA HARAWAY · 1985

Studying an image of Sojourner Truth once more, as I prepared to render my own index finger temporarily inoperable, I noticed that Truth did not avoid the use of her missing finger; she adapted to it. When performing the basic knit stitch, one uses the tips of both pointer fingers to push the yarn into place, pick up stitches to move them to the other needle, to create loops and to tighten up the thread. In one of the photos she had commissioned I noticed that the yarn was resting on the remaining portion of her index finger, and positioned in the space between her index and fingers. Did Sojourner Truth, in adapting to her circumstances, become a form of cyborg? By Donna Haraway’s definition in “A Manifesto for Cyborgs: Science, Technology, and Socialist Feminism for the 1980s,” “A cyborg is a cybernetic organism, a hybrid of machine and organism, a creature of social reality as well as a creature of fiction” (Haraway, 1985). Enslaved people were not taught the skill of knitting. The adaptation of learning to knit was an outward expression of her humanity. Using the tools of knitting; the needles themselves, Truth engaged with technology as a cybernetic organism that advanced her humanity.

I recognized taping up my own finger as an act of Agential Realism (Barad, 2007) in order to address the conversation of intre-relationship with Truth. I also discovered in making my right index finger inoperable, my body engaged, sympathically, to assist in the task of knitting. Barad discusses touch in the talk, “On Touching the Stranger Within – The Alterity that therefore I Am” – An Experimental Lecture, revealing that, “so much happens in a touch: an infinity of others — other beings, other spaces, other times — are aroused” (Barad, 2021). That intra-action can be felt within the same body as well. I didn’t have to ask my body to help me solve the problem of knitting with one less finger. My body crossed gaps and spaces to offer support. I imagine that Truth learned knitting, not only to “prove” her humanity, but to also self-regulate through touch in the way one might use the hands to form a mudra, in the spirit of ancient Hindu and Buddhist traditions.

To deepen my experiment with affected knitting, I experimented with the sound. I added contact mics to the base of two metal knitting needles, plugged them into a sound board, running the sound through several guitar pedals. How does the struggle to knit sound in my hands? I listened for collisions of the needles, where having two index fingers would have rendered a smoother production of the sound. I experimented with the length of the sound waves produced by the needles, seeking to engage with time and its effects on engagements. Knitting, a usually quiet pastime, was hypersonic, highlighting its violence. Knitting with sound was at times percussive, frustrating, and exhilarating. I was only able to knit with two needles. I had to imagine the sound of Sojourner Truth, creating her hat, which would have required five straight needles to accomplish. Several of her photos include Truth knitting in the round, a true feat of human/cyborg engineering.

The photograph was revolutionary.

Truth’s lifePhotography

While I mentioned that Truth commissioned several photos of her in the act of knitting, it should be remarked upon that Truth was engaging with a second form of technology: the photograph was revolutionary. Modern photography advanced in leaps and bounds from the days of the daguerreotype which only allowed one photographic image to be produced per tinotype in the 1830s. To the creation of carte de visite in the 1850s that ushered in the industrialization of photography. This allowed for an almost quantum expression of images. Through Truth’s vision to defy the notion of being what Hurston calls creatures of task alone to an important, embodied subject of photography, she shared versions of her that made visible the permutations of a Black woman’s humanity, through time.
